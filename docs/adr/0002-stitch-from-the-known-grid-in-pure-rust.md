# The Stitch starts from the known grid, in pure Rust, on the CPU

The experimental Stitch (GLOSSARY) assembles a completed Session's Shots into a Panorama inside Panowww, in the `panowww-stitch` crate: no OS code, no `unsafe`, no OpenCV, no GPL component, `rayon` for the threads. Three choices behind it are hard to undo and would surprise a reader who knows how PTGui or Hugin work.

**The grid is the prior, measured against the Shots.** A Session is a uniform grid: `shotsPerRow` Shots per full turn, `rows` from pole to pole. Where a general-purpose Stitcher detects features in every image and matches every pair, the Stitch compares each Shot only with its four grid neighbours, by patch correlation in a common view, from an eighth of the resolution up. It then fits a **gimbal model**: one pitch per Row, one yaw per Shot, one focal length, no roll, with weak priors towards the grid and a tight yaw prior on the pole Rows, followed by a small free rotation per Shot. Nothing from the grid is trusted as such. The spike of 2026-11-13 and 2026-11-14 met, on four real Sessions, every way a capture departs from its plan: a camera that never reached the Nadir (World of Warcraft, Rows from −71° to +80° instead of −90° to +90°); a turn Step of 26.7° instead of the planned 25.7°, and another alternating 44° and 55° from one Step to the next (NoClip); Rows starting 29° to 41° further round than the Row below, so the closing Step is nothing like the grid's; a Step the game swallowed, leaving two identical consecutive Shots and the rest of the Row one Step behind. Each of these broke a version of the alignment that trusted the grid more than the Shots, so two stages are mandatory, not optimisations: **dropped-Step detection** before matching (identical consecutive Shots pull the grid prior of the Row's later Shots back one Step), and **Row placement** before any fine matching (for each Row, from the horizon Row outwards, the heading over the full turn and the pitch over ±20° that make it agree with its placed neighbour, verified by the fine matcher's accepted-match count). After that, the priors of the adjustment damp each pass around its starting estimate; they never pull a Shot back towards the raw grid. A Shot without control points is placed from the grid with its neighbours' measured values, never dropped. We chose this over free three-axis rotations per Shot because flat ground (asphalt, grass) correlates at any roll and let the pole Row collapse; the gimbal parametrisation cannot. The cost: a Session that is not a gimbal capture (a camera that rolls, or orbits) is out of the model; orbit cameras show as parallax at the seams, which the seam finder handles, not the alignment.

**Pure Rust.** `image`, `nalgebra`, `rayon`, `img-parts` for the XMP segment, nothing else. OpenCV would have to be built and bundled for macOS and Windows (20–40 MB per installer, LLVM on CI, `unsafe` FFI); Hugin's `nona`, `enblend` and `libpano13` are GPL and cannot ship inside an MIT app. The crates that exist for feature detection in Rust are young or dormant; the known grid lets us need none of them. The cost: the remapping, the seam finder and the multi-band blend are our own code.

**CPU first, the render stage behind one seam.** PTGui's GPU work is remapping and blending; its time scales with output size. On the spike, 98 Shots of 1669 × 841 become a 5184 × 2592 Panorama in 3.2 s on a 14-thread Mac (PTGui 13: 16.1 s on the same Session), the multi-band render being 2.4 s of it. That is fast enough for the Shots Panowww captures today, so there is no `wgpu`, no shader, no video memory to manage on two OSes. The render stage (labels, seams, blend) lives behind a single internal seam so that a GPU implementation can replace it later without touching the alignment, if 4K Shots push a render past a few seconds. The cost: a 36 × 36 grid at 4K renders in tens of seconds, by bands, within a memory budget.

Decided on the 2026-11-13 spike and the 2026-11-14 architecture conversation, the grid lessons added after the sparse-grid Session of 2026-11-14; measurements and dead ends in the spike's README (`prototype/stitch-spike/`, not published) and the research notes under `docs/research/`.
